\begin{abstract}
We compare MobileNetV2, ShuffleNetV2, and EfficientNet-B0 for poultry-fecal disease classification on resource-constrained edge devices. The audit of the published 8,770-image benchmark finds 617 near-duplicate pairs, including 94 test images byte-identical to training images; results therefore use an 8,153-image deduplicated split. Dynamic INT8 changes MobileNetV2 and ShuffleNetV2 only slightly but collapses EfficientNet-B0 to 10.42\%. Static per-channel QDQ recovers EfficientNet-B0 to 94.56\%, while selective ablation indicates strong sensitivity to depthwise-layer granularity. On Raspberry Pi~5, dynamic INT8 is 1.93--2.80$\times$ slower than FP32 at four threads, whereas static per-channel QDQ reaches 94.56\% at 13.50\,ms, 2.34$\times$ faster than FP32. These results show that deployment outcomes depend jointly on architecture, runtime, and quantization granularity.
\end{abstract}

deep learning, poultry disease detection, lightweight CNN, quantization, edge computing, post-training quantization

\section{Introduction}

Poultry production is a major agricultural sector in the United States (combined broiler, egg, turkey, and chicken value of \$67.1~billion in 2023~\cite{usda2024}). Coccidiosis, Salmonellosis, and Newcastle Disease (NCD) cause major losses~\cite{machuve2022}. Fecal image analysis enables noninvasive early detection, but laboratory diagnosis remains inaccessible to many small farms~\cite{tasdelen2025}.

Recent deep learning approaches have achieved high accuracy for poultry fecal disease classification. Dhungana~\textit{et al.}~reported 99.12\% accuracy using EfficientNet-B0~\cite{dhungana2025}, while Tasdelen and Arslan achieved 97.1\% with MobileNetV2~\cite{tasdelen2025}. However, these solutions primarily target cloud-based deployment, leaving a critical gap: \textit{how can these models be efficiently deployed on resource-constrained edge devices at farm level?}

Lightweight CNNs---MobileNetV2~\cite{mobilenetv2}, ShuffleNetV2~\cite{shufflenetv2}, and EfficientNet~\cite{efficientnet}---are candidates for edge deployment because they need fewer operations. INT8 post-training quantization (PTQ) reduces size by using 8-bit weights~\cite{jacob2018}. Even on CPUs that expose INT8 acceleration, PTQ can increase latency rather than reduce it when the runtime kernel path adds quantize/dequantize work around every convolution; our x86 reference (12th Gen i5-12400F, AVX-VNNI present per \texttt{/proc/cpuinfo}) still shows a 3.0--16.8$\times$ slowdown, so the slowdown is consistent with the ORT CPU kernel path adding quantize/dequantize work, not raw ISA absence; kernel-level attribution is discussed cautiously in Sec.~IV-C. See also the quantization white paper for why PTQ without calibration degrades on depthwise layers~\cite{nagel2021}.

This paper addresses two research questions: (1) What is the quantization sensitivity of each lightweight architecture under INT8 PTQ on CPU, and where exactly does EfficientNet-B0 fail? (2) What is the accuracy-latency tradeoff for these models under INT8 PTQ on a Raspberry Pi~5? We additionally audit the dataset integrity claim underlying prior work on this benchmark.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows. First, we audit the FecalFed leak-free claim~\cite{chi2026} and identify 617 near-duplicate pairs, including 393 byte-exact pairs and 94 test images byte-identical to training images; all results use the resulting 8,153-image deduplicated split with 5,100/1,325/1,728 train/validation/test images and zero cross-split survivors. Second, we characterize EfficientNet-B0 sensitivity to quantization granularity: dynamic INT8 and static per-tensor QDQ collapse to 10.42\% and 9.32\%, respectively, while static per-channel QDQ recovers 94.56\%. Selective ablations and layerwise SQNR provide evidence that depthwise layers are especially sensitive in this configuration, but do not establish a universal necessary-and-sufficient mechanism. Third, we characterize the three models on Raspberry Pi~5 across 12 model--quantization--thread configurations and compare dynamic with static QDQ, showing that deployment latency depends on the runtime and quantization scheme.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Poultry Disease Detection}
Machuve~\textit{et al.}~introduced the foundational dataset of 6,812 PCR-verified fecal images from Tanzanian poultry farms~\cite{machuve2022}. Subsequent work has applied various deep learning architectures: Dhungana~\textit{et al.}~combined YOLOv11 detection with EfficientNet-B0 classification, achieving 99.12\% accuracy and 25.8\,ms inference~\cite{dhungana2025}. Tasdelen and Arslan benchmarked six architectures, finding MobileNetV2 optimal for both accuracy (97.1\%) and training speed~\cite{tasdelen2025}. Degu and Simegn employed YOLO-V3 for region-of-interest detection followed by ResNet50 classification~\cite{degu2023}. Chi introduced FecalFed, a federated-learning benchmark on this dataset family claiming 8,770 rigorously deduplicated unique images~\cite{chi2026}; our audit in Section~III-A tests that claim directly. Prior work on this dataset has not compared lightweight models under quantization with accuracy-latency tradeoff analysis.

\subsection{Lightweight CNN Architectures}
MobileNetV2~\cite{mobilenetv2} utilizes inverted residuals with linear bottlenecks and depthwise separable convolutions for efficient feature extraction. ShuffleNetV2~\cite{shufflenetv2} introduces channel shuffle operations for cross-channel information flow with only 1.26M parameters. EfficientNet~\cite{efficientnet} employs compound scaling to uniformly balance network depth, width, and resolution. Hoang~\textit{et al.}~recently compared seven lightweight architectures for plant disease detection, identifying hybrid designs as optimal for edge deployment~\cite{hoang2026}.

\subsection{Model Quantization}
INT8 quantization reduces model size by representing weights with 8-bit integers~\cite{jacob2018}. While theoretically achieving 4$\times$ compression, ONNX Runtime documentation~\cite{onnxruntime2024} explicitly notes that INT8 without matching kernel support may degrade performance due to quantize/dequantize overhead. The white paper on quantization surveys why PTQ without calibration degrades on depthwise and SE layers~\cite{nagel2021}.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Dataset}
We use the Machuve~\textit{et al.}~dataset~\cite{machuve22} with PCR-verified fecal images from Tanzanian farms, as distributed via HuggingFace~\texttt{Dianyo/poultry-fecal-fl}. Chi~\cite{chi2026} describes this benchmark as 8,770 rigorously deduplicated leak-free images with train/test splits of 7,016/1,754. We audit this claim with pHash (Hamming $\le$ 5) over all 8,770 files and find 617 near-duplicate pairs (393 byte-exact), including 303 groups spanning splits: 94 test images byte-identical to train images and 31 to validation images.

All numbers in this paper are therefore reported on a deduplicated sterile split: we drop one copy per duplicate pair (keeping test $>$ val $>$ train), yielding 8,153 unique images with 5,100/1,325/1,728 train/val/test and zero cross-split survivors under this audit (audit artifact \texttt{results/phash\_dedup.json}, split manifest \texttt{results/sterile\_split\_manifest.json}). Concretely: the audit found 617 near-duplicate pairs (all size 2, class-consistent; 393 byte-exact), of which 303 groups span splits; we drop exactly one copy per pair keeping test $>$ val $>$ train (within a split, lexicographically-first kept), so counts refer to removed copies rather than connected groups. The validation split was carved as a stratified 80/20 split of the publisher train only (seed 42); publisher test was never re-split. We use the shorthand \emph{sterile split} throughout for this pHash-deduplicated partition. The term denotes only the absence of cross-split near-duplicate pairs detected by the audit described above; it is not a claim of independence between images, since residual dependence may remain within a split. We note as a limitation that this removes leakage but does not rebuild a fresh split from the Zenodo originals (6,812 images); the sterile test set remains 98\% inherited from the publisher test split. Classes are Coccidiosis, Healthy, Salmonella, and Newcastle Disease; NCD is underrepresented (417/5,100 train, 141/1,728 test), so we report per-class INT8 recall: Coccidiosis 2.27\%, Healthy 4.76\%, Salmonella 0.54\%, and Newcastle Disease 100\%. Images are resized to 224$\times$224 and normalized with ImageNet mean/std.

\subsection{Model Architectures}
We evaluate three ImageNet~\cite{deng2009}-pretrained lightweight architectures with the classification head replaced for four classes (3.4M$\rightarrow$2.2M for MobileNetV2 after the head swap; parameter counts below are for our 4-class models). MobileNetV2~\cite{mobilenetv2} (2.2M parameters) uses inverted residuals with depthwise separable convolutions for efficient local feature extraction. ShuffleNetV2~\cite{shufflenetv2} (1.3M parameters) relies on channel shuffle for cross-channel mixing with few parameters. EfficientNet-B0~\cite{efficientnet} (4.0M parameters) applies compound scaling with squeeze-and-excitation blocks to capture multi-scale features.

\subsection{Training Protocol}
All models are ImageNet-pretrained (torchvision V1 weights) with only the classification head replaced (4 classes); all layers are trainable (no freezing). Each model is trained once (seed 42) for 50 epochs using Adam (learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$) with cosine annealing; the checkpoint with the best validation accuracy is kept. Batch size is 32, input resolution $224 \times 224$. Data augmentation (train only): random horizontal flip, rotation ($\pm 15^\circ$), color jitter (brightness/contrast/saturation 0.2); eval preprocessing is resize$\to$tensor$\to$ImageNet normalize. Training is performed on an NVIDIA RTX 4060 GPU with 8\,GB VRAM using PyTorch 2.5.1 with CUDA 12.1.

\subsection{Quantization and Benchmarking (x86)}
Models are exported to ONNX (opset 13) and quantized with ONNX Runtime dynamic per-tensor INT8 using signed QInt8 weights and dynamically quantized activations; default operator selection includes convolution operators observed in the resulting graphs. The original static-QDQ run used the first 100 training images sequentially; because the dataset loader is class-ordered, this calibration set contained only Coccidiosis images and was not class-balanced. We therefore treat the static-QDQ accuracy as a single-slice diagnostic result and list stratified recalibration as required follow-up, not as a claim of a generally calibrated deployment. Static QDQ uses signed QInt8 weights and activations, MinMax calibration, per-tensor or per-channel weight scales, and standard ORT quantization preprocessing; no custom graph fusion is enabled. The native-Linux rerun, performed with the same ORT model-only protocol, gives dynamic-INT8 slowdowns of 14.73--14.97$\times$ for MobileNetV2, 3.22--3.26$\times$ for ShuffleNetV2, and 14.08--14.08$\times$ for EfficientNet-B0 at one and four threads, respectively. These host-reference values are reported separately from the WSL and Raspberry Pi~5 results.

\subsection{Edge Deployment on Raspberry Pi~5}
We characterize the same ONNX FP32 and INT8 models on a Raspberry Pi~5 (Broadcom BCM2712, 4$\times$ Cortex-A76 at up to 2.4\,GHz, 8\,GB LPDDR4X, Debian 13 trixie, kernel 6.18.34+rpt-rpi-2712, aarch64, \texttt{ondemand} governor, throttled=0x0). ONNX Runtime 1.29.0 is used with only \texttt{CPUExecutionProvider} (no NPU/GPU; \texttt{AzureExecutionProvider} listed but unused). The sterile-model sweep (\texttt{scripts/benchmark\_pi\_gate0.py}) covers 12 configurations: 3 models $\times$ 2 quantizations (FP32/INT8 dynamic) $\times$ 2 thread counts (1 and 4 via \texttt{intra\_op\_num\_threads}) $\times$ batch 1, with input 3$\times$224$\times$224. Each configuration runs 200 iterations after 10 warmup iterations (single session per config), reporting mean/std/P50/P95/P99 latency, throughput, RSS memory (mean/peak), CPU\%, and die temperature before/after. Variability reported is within-session; variability across independent benchmark sessions was not measured. All timings are model-only (eval preprocessing and data transfer excluded), not end-to-end application throughput. Initial temperature was 44.6$^{\circ}$C; peak during the sweep was 55.6$^{\circ}$C. Current/power telemetry was unavailable (\texttt{current\_a=null}) on this board, so energy is discussed as a limitation. All 12/12 runs completed without thermal throttling.

EfficientNet-B0 has the highest FP32 accuracy (98.55\%), but dynamic INT8 collapses to 10.42\%; Fig.~\ref{fig:cm} shows that 1,676/1,728 predictions fall in the Newcastle Disease column. Static per-tensor QDQ also collapses to 9.32\%, whereas per-channel QDQ recovers 94.56\% (Table~\ref{tab:eb0static}). \subsection{Raspberry Pi~5 Characterization: Thread Scaling and the INT8 Bottleneck}
Table~\ref{tab:rpi5static} and Table~\ref{tab:rpi5} summarize the sterile-model Raspberry Pi~5 latency at batch=1. Fig.~\ref{fig:rpi5thread} visualizes the thread-scaling contrast. FP32 scales with threads: MobileNetV2 drops from 33.16\,ms (1 thread, 30.2\,FPS) to 14.23\,ms (4 threads, 70.3\,FPS, 2.33$\times$ speedup); ShuffleNetV2 from 14.19\,ms to 6.19\,ms (2.29$\times$, 161.5\,FPS); EfficientNet-B0 from 69.45\,ms to 31.56\,ms (2.20$\times$). The sweep covers threads 1 and 4; intermediate thread counts are not measured for the sterile models.

The dynamic INT8 slowdown reported in Table~\ref{tab:rpi5} is a property of dynamic quantization on this runtime, not of INT8 in general. Table~\ref{tab:rpi5static} repeats the EfficientNet-B0 measurement on the same board, same ORT build, and same session with the static QDQ graphs whose accuracy we characterized in Table~\ref{tab:eb0static}. Static QDQ is 2.31--2.34$\times$ \emph{faster} than FP32 at 4 threads (13.63 and 13.50\,ms versus 31.53\,ms), reversing the direction of the dynamic result (63.99\,ms, 0.49$\times$ of FP32). The static per-channel result is faster and more accurate in this experiment, but it does not establish that granularity always dominates bit width: the comparison uses one board, one runtime, one seed, and one calibration set, and leaving depthwise layers in FP32 reaches 97.16\%.

\textbf{Measured bottleneck (Level-2).} Table~\ref{tab:level2} quantifies why INT8 is slower. Dynamic PTQ does not insert static QuantizeLinear/DequantizeLinear nodes; it expands each Conv (52--81) into a DynamicQuantizeLinear + ConvInteger + Mul (scale) + Cast pattern, so node count grows by 1.37--3.05$\times$ on the sterile graphs. ORT kernel profiling on RPi5 (enable\_profiling, 15+5 runs) shows dispatched kernels: FP32 has zero quantization kernels, while INT8 dynamic dispatches ConvInteger 51--58\%, DynamicQuantizeLinear 9--15\%, and Mul/Add/Cast 20--35\% combined; all quantization-related kernels add 20.7--29.2\% overhead. The slowdown ranking agrees with the measured overhead ranking for these three models, but the sample is too small to claim a general expansion-overhead correlation. CPU feature detection exposes ARM dot-product support (\texttt{asimddp}) but not \texttt{i8mm} or SVE; whether the MLAS kernels use the dot-product instructions was not verified. Artifact \texttt{kernel\_dispatch\_artifact.json} contains the per-operator timings and operation counts, complementing TinyML system studies~\cite{david2021}.

\subsection{Accuracy-Latency Tradeoff}

The accuracy-latency comparison shows that ShuffleNetV2 FP32 is fastest at 6.19\,ms on RPi5 (161.5\,FPS), EfficientNet-B0 is most accurate at 98.55\%, and MobileNetV2 FP32 is intermediate (97.92\%, 14.23\,ms). The gap between best and worst FP32 accuracy is only 0.86pp (97.69\% versus 98.55\%); hardware constraints and quantization scheme should drive model choice for deployment. By ``balanced'' we mean MobileNetV2 FP32 sits between the latency extremes (14.23\,ms vs 6.19\,ms ShuffleNetV2 and 31.56\,ms EfficientNet-B0 at 4 threads) while staying within 0.63pp of the best accuracy. All latency numbers are means of 200 runs after 10 warmup iterations (batch 1, model-only). On x86, ShuffleNetV2 FP32 (4.15\,ms) is slower than MobileNetV2 FP32 (2.40\,ms) but the order flips on RPi5 (6.19\,ms vs 14.23\,ms at 4 threads); reference and deployment hardware rank architectures differently, which is why deployment claims rest on RPi5 only.


\subsection{Comparison with Related Work}

Our accuracy (98.55\%) is competitive with prior results on the same dataset family, although the protocols differ in splits, deduplication, and metrics, so this is not a head-to-head benchmark. Our added analysis in quantization diagnosis and accuracy-latency tradeoff characterization under INT8 is absent from prior work.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper characterizes lightweight CNN architectures with INT8 quantization for poultry fecal disease detection. FP32 accuracy exceeds 97\% for all models; dynamic INT8 yields 97.28\% for MobileNetV2 and 97.63\% for ShuffleNetV2, but collapses EfficientNet-B0 to 10.42\%. Static per-channel QDQ recovers EfficientNet-B0 to 94.56\%, while the EfficientNet-B0 ablation indicates strong sensitivity to depthwise-layer granularity (93.46\% when depthwise layers alone are per-channel versus 8.33\% when they are not). Dynamic INT8 is 3.0--16.8$\times$ slower on x86 and 1.93--2.80$\times$ slower on RPi5 at four threads, whereas static per-channel QDQ is 2.34$\times$ faster than FP32 on RPi5. ShuffleNetV2 FP32 is fastest on RPi5 at 6.19\,ms, while MobileNetV2 FP32 provides an intermediate latency of 14.23\,ms with 97.92\% accuracy.

This paper characterizes one board, one runtime build, one dataset, one random seed, and one 100-image calibration set; multi-seed confidence intervals, stratified recalibration, and calibration-size sweeps are not included in the reported tables. Energy measurement was unavailable, and the RPi5 latency measurements were model-only at batch 1. The selective quantization ablation was performed only for EfficientNet-B0; extending it to MobileNetV2 and ShuffleNetV2 would test whether the observed depthwise sensitivity generalizes. Quantization-aware training and class-imbalance mitigation for the underrepresented NCD class also remain open.
